# Reviewer 1

## Overall Comment

This is a very interesting and exceptional study, and merits publication. The authors note that "Kumamoto offers a rare setting in which official records connect an earlier transport failure, a completed intervention, a later earthquake, and a subsequent service observation. The 2016 Kumamoto earthquake sequence, foreshock 14 April 2016, M_JMA 6.5; mainshock 16 April 2016, M_JMA 7.3, damaged transport assets across the prefecture, and a second M_JMA 7.1 earthquake struck the same region on 28 July 2026, after several of the resulting reconstruction and capacity projects had reached completion."

The elapse time of a decade between the two large mainshocks is opportune for the conduct of the study that the authors have undertaken. The authors have rightly taken advantage of this opportunity.

To improve this paper, the following suggestions are made:

## Comment 2

[B] The authors note rightly that the principal identification limit is the absence of a credible counterfactual. This paper addresses a single sequence of seismic events. How would the inferences have differed if there were some variation in the seismic sequence?
This raises the question as to what else might have happened? Suppose that the second major earthquake had been larger, e.g. M7.5? To support the robustness of the conclusions, the authors might explore some downward counterfactuals - alternative realizations of the past where things turned for the worse. Under what circumstances might there have been serious damage in the 2026 mainshock?

The usefulness of this paper, and its generalization to other seismic environments, would be enhanced by broadening the context of this study to include such downward counterfactuals.

**Response:**
Thank you for this comment. The revised Discussion examines how greater asset damage, corridor obstruction and further shocks during inspection and repair could alter the observed reopening and passage findings. It also explains why the M7.5 example requires asset-level demand assumptions and describes these adverse sequences as conditional pathways rather than numerical predictions.

"A hypothetical M7.5 event does not by itself specify demand at either asset; rupture geometry, distance and site conditions also matter. At Yokoe, stronger component-relevant shaking or greater ground deformation could cause extensive foundation or retained-component damage, increasing repair needs and weakening or reversing the reopening contrast. At Mashiki, debris or ground damage blocking the available carriageways or their connections could interrupt passage despite widening. Further strong shocks before inspection and repair are complete could compound damage or renew closures in either case. These downward counterfactuals identify conditions under which the observed restoration and passage findings may cease to transfer; they do not estimate outcomes under an alternative sequence."
(Pages 12–13, Lines 259–269)

**Response:**
Thank you for this comment. The revised Methods define longitudinal coverage, explain the 0.80 cutoff as a descriptive classification that permits up to 20% of the longitudinal span to remain unrepresented, and specify the alternative-cutoff comparison with trace extraction held fixed.

"Longitudinal coverage is the union length of selected trace intervals on the polygon's longitudinal axis divided by its projected span, capped at one. The 0.80 cutoff permits up to 20% of this span to remain unrepresented; it is a descriptive classification, not a calibrated lane-availability or capacity threshold.
